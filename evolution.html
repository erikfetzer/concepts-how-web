Evolution · how life changes

No designer, just survivors.

Living things vary, some of that variation is passed on, and the variants that happen to survive and reproduce more leave more copies of themselves. Repeat for billions of years and you get every creature that has ever lived. Play the hungry bird, watch luck steer small populations, see how tiny steps add up, and find out how long ago you shared a great-great-…-grandparent with a mushroom.

Demo 1 · be the bird

Natural selection: hide or be eaten

Peppered moths rest on tree bark, where birds hunt them by sight. When soot from factories blackened the trees of industrial Britain, dark moths became far more common; when air got cleaner, pale moths came back. Tap the moths you can spot. Once you’ve eaten half, the survivors breed, and their young look like their parents, with small random differences. Then switch the bark and see what happens.

What really happenedThe first dark peppered moth was recorded near Manchester in 1848. By 1895 about 98% of moths there were dark. After clean-air laws, pale moths became common again. In the 2000s Michael Majerus released nearly 4,900 moths and confirmed that birds ate more of the easy-to-spot ones.
Demo 2 · pure luck

Genetic drift: evolution by accident

Not every change is about survival. Imagine a gene comes in two versions, blue and orange, and neither is better. Each generation, which parents happen to have more children is partly random. In a small population those accidents add up fast: one version can vanish completely and the other takes over. Each line below is a separate population starting at 50% orange.

All orange
Orange lost
Still mixed
Small groups, big swingsDrift is strongest when few individuals breed. It helps explain why isolated islands and small founding groups often have unusual traits. Cheetahs are so genetically alike that skin grafts between unrelated cheetahs can take, likely because their numbers crashed long ago.
Demo 3 · small steps add up

Monkeys at a keyboard

People sometimes say evolution is like a monkey typing Shakespeare by chance. But selection isn’t one giant lucky guess. In 1986 Richard Dawkins showed the difference with a toy program. Random typing almost never hits the target. Keeping the best copy each round, and letting it make slightly wrong copies of itself, gets there in a few dozen generations. It’s not a model of real evolution, which has no target, but it shows how keeping small wins changes everything.

The odds of chanceWith 27 possible keys and characters, a random guess has about a 1 in chance of being right. Even a computer making a billion guesses a second since the Big Bang would almost certainly still be going.
Demo 4 · evolution right now

How bacteria beat antibiotics

Bacteria can double in under an hour, so evolution happens fast enough to watch. In any large group, a few bacteria are naturally a bit tougher. An antibiotic kills the weak ones and leaves the tough ones with all the space and food. Give a dose, let the survivors regrow, and repeat. Redder dots are more resistant.

Why it mattersAntibiotics don’t work on viruses such as colds and flu, and using them when they aren’t needed gives resistant bacteria more chances to win. Drug-resistant infections are now one of the world’s biggest health threats. If you are prescribed antibiotics, take them exactly as your doctor or pharmacist says.
Demo 5 · your extended family

The tree of life

Every living thing is related. Pick a creature to see roughly when its ancestors and yours were the same population. Dates come from comparing DNA and fossils, and scientists still refine them, so treat them as rough estimates. The time axis is squashed so recent splits are easier to see.

Not from chimpsHumans didn’t evolve from chimpanzees. We and chimps are cousins: both descend from an ape that lived in Africa about 6 to 7 million years ago. Chimps have been evolving for exactly as long as we have.
