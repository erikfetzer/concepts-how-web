Codes & encryption · secrets, keys, hashes

Hide a message in plain sight, then break it.

For thousands of years people have scrambled messages so only the right reader can understand them, and for just as long others have tried to break them. Today the same ideas protect every card payment, message and password. Spin a cipher wheel, crack a secret with letter counting, watch a hash explode, and share a secret in front of an eavesdropper.

The Caesar cipher

Julius Caesar shifted every letter three places along the alphabet: A becomes D, B becomes E, and so on. The amount of shift is the key. Turn the inner wheel and type a message.

Encrypted
How strong is it?There are only 25 useful keys, so anyone can try them all in a minute. That’s a brute-force attack. Modern ciphers have more possible keys than there are atoms in the observable universe.

Crack it with letter counting

Around 850 CE the Arab scholar al-Kindi described frequency analysis: in any long text some letters are far more common than others. In English, E is the most common. Hide the letters behind a shift and the pattern survives, just slid along. Line up the bars to find the key.

Demo 3 · a keyword

The Vigenère cipher

Use a keyword instead of one shift: each letter of the key sets the shift for one letter of the message, repeating. The same letter can now encrypt to many different letters, which flattens the frequency pattern. It was called “the indecipherable cipher” for three centuries, until Charles Babbage and Friedrich Kasiski broke it in the 1800s.

The perfect cipherIf the key is truly random, as long as the message, and never reused, the result is a one-time pad. Claude Shannon proved in 1949 that it can’t be broken, even in principle. The catch is sharing all that key safely.
Demo 4 · fingerprints

Hashing: a one-way fingerprint

A hash function turns any input, a word or a whole film, into a fixed-size fingerprint. SHA-256 always gives 256 bits. You can’t run it backwards, and changing a single character changes about half of the bits. That’s how sites check passwords without storing them, and how blockchains link blocks.

The 256 bits, side by side
Bits that changed
The avalanche effectEach red square is a bit that flipped. A good hash flips about 128 of 256 for any change, so similar inputs give completely unrelated fingerprints and nobody can work backwards by trial and adjustment.
Demo 5 · the impossible trick

Agree on a secret while everyone listens

How can two strangers agree on a secret key when every message they send is overheard? In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman showed how. The classic way to picture it is with paint: mixing is easy, unmixing is practically impossible.

The real thing uses numbers
Why the eavesdropper is stuckEve sees 5, 23 and both public numbers. To get the secret she must find a from 5a mod 23. With tiny numbers that’s easy, but real systems use numbers hundreds of digits long, and no known method can do it in any reasonable time.
Demo 6 · public keys

A padlock anyone can close

In 1977 Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman published RSA: a public key that anyone can use to lock a message, and a private key that only you can use to open it. Its security rests on one fact: multiplying two big primes is easy, but splitting the result back into them is fiendishly hard. Here it is with tiny primes.

    Real keysReal RSA uses primes over 300 digits long, so n has more than 600 digits. Your browser uses public-key cryptography like this every time you see the padlock next to a web address.
