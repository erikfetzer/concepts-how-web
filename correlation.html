Correlation vs causation · together isn’t the same as because

Ice cream doesn’t cause shark attacks.

Two things are correlated when they tend to go up and down together. That’s useful, but it doesn’t tell you that one causes the other. Maybe it’s the other way round, maybe something else drives both, or maybe it’s pure chance. Drag dots to make correlations, watch unrelated trends line up, find the hidden third factor and run an experiment that can actually show cause.

Demo 1 · measuring togetherness

What a correlation looks like

The correlation coefficient, r, runs from −1 to +1. Near +1, when one goes up the other almost always goes up; near −1, one goes up as the other goes down; near 0, there’s no straight-line pattern. Drag the dots, tap empty space to add one, or try a preset. Watch how a single outlier can swing r, and how a clear curve can still give r near 0.

Storks and babiesIn 2000 statistician Robert Matthews showed that across 17 European countries, the number of breeding stork pairs correlated with the number of babies born. Bigger countries have more of both. He published it to teach exactly this lesson.
Demo 3 · the hidden third factor

Ice cream and shark sightings

On days when a beach sells more ice cream, lifeguards report more sharks. Should we ban ice cream? No: hot weather drives both, since more people buy ice cream and more people swim. A hidden cause like this is called a confounder. This is simulated data for 200 days. Turn on “same temperature only” to compare just days with similar weather, and the link mostly vanishes.

Real examplesChildren with bigger feet read better (age drives both). Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel prizes per person (wealth is a likely confounder). People who carry lighters get more lung cancer (smoking).

Demo 5 · how to actually prove cause

Let a coin decide

Does a homework app raise test scores? If you just compare students who chose the app with those who didn’t, keen students are more likely to pick it, and they’d score higher anyway. In a randomised trial a coin flip decides who gets the app, so keenness ends up equal in both groups and any difference left is caused by the app. You set the app’s true effect; see which study finds it.

When trials changed medicineFor years, observational studies suggested hormone therapy protected women’s hearts. Women who chose it tended to be healthier anyway. When a large randomised trial reported in 2002, it found no heart benefit and some increased risks, and advice changed.
