Language · words, roots, writing

Three, tres, drei, trí: a family resemblance.

About 7,000 languages are spoken today, and every one is a complete system that lets people say anything. Many are related: English and Spanish are distant cousins that share a common ancestor spoken some 6,000 years ago. Trace words back to their roots, find the hidden maths in how we use words, write your name in other scripts and dodge the false friends.

One word, many languages

In 1786 Sir William Jones noticed that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin were too alike to be a coincidence. They all descend from one lost language, Proto-Indo-European, now reconstructed from its descendants. Pick a word and see how it changed as the family spread from India to Ireland. A star (*) marks a reconstructed word nobody ever wrote down.

Grimm’s lawJacob Grimm, of the fairy tales, described in 1822 how Germanic languages shifted certain sounds in regular ways. P became F (Latin pater, English father), T became TH (tres, three), K became H (cornu, horn). Sound changes follow rules, which is how linguists can rebuild lost words.

Zipf’s law: the most common word rules

In almost any long text, the most common word appears about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on. George Kingsley Zipf popularised this pattern in the 1930s and 40s. It holds across languages and nobody fully knows why. The text box starts with the words on this page; paste anything you like.

Why it mattersA few hundred words make up about half of everyday text, while most words are rare. That’s why learning the 1,000 most common words of a language gets you surprisingly far, and why spell-checkers and predictive keyboards work.
Demo 3 · writing

Your name, written other ways

Writing was invented independently at least three or four times: in Mesopotamia, Egypt (perhaps), China and Mesoamerica. Our alphabet came from Egyptian-inspired signs used by Semitic speakers around 1800 BCE, then passed through Phoenician, Greek and Latin. Type a name to see it in some of the world’s other systems.

How the letter A got its shapeAround 1800 BCE the first alphabet borrowed an Egyptian picture of an ox’s head for the sound at the start of ʾalp, “ox”. The Phoenicians wrote it as aleph, the Greeks turned it into alpha, and over the centuries it rotated until the horns pointed down: A. Tip it upside down and you can still see the ox.
BrailleLouis Braille, blinded as a child, published his six-dot system in 1829 at the age of 20. Each letter is a pattern in a 2 × 3 grid of raised dots, read by touch.

True friends, too

Many English words ending in -tion become -ción in Spanish (nation, nación), -ty often becomes -dad (city, ciudad; reality, realidad), and -ous becomes -oso (famous, famoso). Thousands of words follow these patterns because both languages borrowed heavily from Latin.

Why they driftWords change meaning over centuries. Spanish embarazada once meant “hindered, encumbered”, and narrowed to “pregnant”. English kept embarrassed for social awkwardness. Same root, different journeys.
