Game theory · strategy, cooperation, equilibrium

Everyone does what’s best for them, and everyone loses.

Game theory is the maths of decisions that depend on other people’s decisions. It explains price wars, arms races, penalty kicks, auctions and why cooperation is both fragile and surprisingly hard to kill. Play the most famous game of all, then watch strategies compete, evolve and settle into equilibrium.

Demo 2 · competition

Axelrod’s tournament

In 1980 the political scientist Robert Axelrod invited experts to submit programs to play the repeated prisoner’s dilemma against each other, 200 rounds a match. The winner was the shortest entry: Tit for Tat, sent in by Anatol Rapoport. Pick the field, add some noise (mistakes), and run it yourself.

Average points per round Evolution: population share over 150 generations
Strategies in the tournament
What winsAxelrod found the successful strategies shared four traits. They were nice (never defect first), retaliatory (punish defection), forgiving (go back to cooperating) and clear (easy for others to read). Turn up the noise and watch unforgiving strategies like Grim Trigger fall apart.
EvolutionIn the lower chart, each strategy’s share of the next generation grows with how well it scored against the current population. Exploiters like Always Defect only thrive while there are naive cooperators to feed on. Remove Grim Trigger and the Tit for Tat family and watch Always Defect take over; add them back and it starves.
Demo 3 · emergence

Cooperation on a grid

In 1992 Martin Nowak and Robert May put the dilemma on a grid. Every cell plays its eight neighbours, then copies whichever neighbour (or itself) scored best. Nobody remembers anything, yet clusters of cooperators can survive, and a single defector can grow into a kaleidoscope.

Demo 4 · equilibrium

Find the Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a set of choices where nobody can do better by changing only their own move. John Nash proved in 1950 that every finite game has at least one, if players are allowed to randomize. Pick a classic game or type your own payoffs.

Blue is the row player’s payoff, red the column player’s. An outlined number is that player’s best response to the other’s move. Where both are outlined, you have a pure Nash equilibrium.

Best responses, if players can randomize

Across: how often the column player picks its first move. Up: how often the row player picks its first move. The blue line is the row player’s best reply, the red line the column player’s. Every crossing is an equilibrium.

Demo 5 · thinking about thinking

Guess two-thirds of the average

Everyone picks a number from 0 to 100. The winner is whoever is closest to two-thirds of the average. If people guess at random the average is 50, so you’d say 33. But if everyone thinks that, you should say 22… The only Nash equilibrium is 0. Does 0 win against real people?

The crowd mixes people who think zero, one, two or three steps ahead, plus a few who go straight to 0. Play several rounds: like real groups, they learn and drift toward 0.

Levels of thinkingLevel 0 guesses at random. Level 1 assumes everyone else is level 0 and says 33. Level 2 says 22, level 3 about 15. In experiments, most people stop after one or two steps, so first-round averages usually land in the 20s or 30s. Being “perfectly rational” and saying 0 loses.
