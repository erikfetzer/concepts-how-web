Money & economics · growth, prices, markets

Money is a story everyone agrees to believe.

A banknote is paper; a bank balance is a number on a computer. They’re worth something only because everyone expects everyone else to accept them. Economics studies how people make choices when things are scarce: why prices rise, how savings grow, where money even comes from. Play with the forces that shape your wallet.

Inflation: why things cost more every year

Inflation is a general rise in prices, which means each unit of money buys less. Most central banks aim for about 2% a year: low enough that money keeps its value, high enough to avoid falling prices, which can freeze spending. Small yearly rates add up, the same way compound interest does.

HyperinflationWhen prices spiral out of control, money becomes worthless fast. In Hungary in 1946, prices doubled roughly every 15 hours, the worst on record. Zimbabwe printed a 100-trillion-dollar note in 2009.

Supply and demand

Buyers want more of something when it’s cheap (demand slopes down). Sellers offer more when they can charge more (supply slopes up). Where the two lines cross is the market price. Shift the curves and watch the price move, then try fixing the price by law.

How banks create money

Most money isn’t coins or notes; it’s numbers in bank accounts. When a bank makes a loan, it simply adds money to the borrower’s account. That money gets spent and deposited in another bank, which lends part of it again. Step through the classic textbook model.

The real world is messierIn practice banks lend first and find reserves later, and how much they lend depends on demand for loans, regulation and their capital. The Bank of England explained this in 2014. In the United States the reserve requirement has been 0% since March 2020. The key point stands: most money is created by banks lending.
