Probability · chance, risk, surprise

Your gut is bad at odds. Let’s prove it.

Probability is the maths of uncertainty: how likely things are, and how to update when you learn something new. It runs weather forecasts, insurance, medicine, games and machine learning. It’s also full of results that feel wrong until you run the experiment yourself, so here you can run them a thousand times.

The Monty Hall problem

A car is behind one door, goats behind the others. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door with a goat. Should you switch? When Marilyn vos Savant said “yes” in 1990, thousands of readers, including mathematicians, wrote in to say she was wrong. She wasn’t.

Your results
Why switching winsYour first pick is right 1 time in 3. The host never opens the car, so all the other 2 in 3 chance gets squeezed into the one door he leaves closed. Try 10 doors: you pick one, he opens eight goats. Would you still stay?

The birthday paradox

How many people do you need in a room for a better-than-even chance that two share a birthday? Most people guess around 180. The answer is 23. The trick: you aren’t looking for someone who shares your birthday, but any pair at all, and the number of pairs grows fast.

The Galton board

Each ball bounces left or right at every peg, completely at random. No single ball is predictable, yet thousands of them always pile into the same smooth bell shape. Francis Galton built one in the 1870s to show how order appears from chance: this is the normal distribution.

The dashed curveis what the maths predicts: the binomial distribution. With many rows it becomes the bell curve, which shows up everywhere sums of many small random effects do: heights, measurement errors, test scores.

Flip a coin a thousand times

The law of large numbers says the share of heads settles toward 50% as flips pile up. But it gets there by averaging, not by “correcting”: a coin has no memory. Watch how long streaks happen far more often than people expect.

The gambler’s fallacyAfter five heads in a row, tails is not “due”. The next flip is still 50/50. In 100 flips, a streak of six or more of the same side happens most of the time.

You tested positive. Should you panic?

A test is 90% accurate and you test positive. Most people say there’s a 90% chance they have the condition. Often the real answer is far lower, because it depends on how rare the condition is. This is Bayes’ theorem, and doctors get it wrong too. Each square below is one of 1,000 people.
