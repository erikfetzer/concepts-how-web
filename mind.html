Biases & the mind · shortcuts, illusions, blind spots

Your brain takes shortcuts. Catch it in the act.

Your brain handles a flood of information with quick rules of thumb. Most of the time they work brilliantly; sometimes they lead you astray in predictable ways. Psychologists call these systematic errors cognitive biases. Test your eyes, your reflexes and your reasoning, then learn the names of the tricks your mind plays.

Fast thinking vs slow thinking

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman described two modes of thought: System 1 is fast, automatic and intuitive; System 2 is slow, effortful and logical. These three puzzles, from Shane Frederick’s 2005 Cognitive Reflection Test, each have an answer that springs to mind instantly, and is wrong.

What’s typicalSimple visual reaction times are usually around 200 to 300 milliseconds. Sounds are a little faster. Tiredness, distraction and alcohol all slow you down, which is why a phone call while driving is dangerous even hands-free.
Demo 4 · interference

The Stroop test

Tap the button for the ink color of each word, ignoring what the word says. First round: the words match their colors. Second round: they don’t. Reading is so automatic you can’t switch it off, and the clash slows you down. John Ridley Stroop published this in 1935, and it is still one of the most repeated experiments in psychology.

How many digits can you hold?

Watch the digits, then type them back in order. Each time you get it right, the list grows by one. In 1956 George Miller wrote a famous paper called “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two” about the limits of short-term memory.

ChunkingMore recent research puts the true limit nearer four “chunks”. The trick is that a chunk can be big: 1-9-1-4 is four items, but “1914” is one. Phone numbers are split into groups for exactly this reason.
Field guide

Cognitive biases

Biases aren’t a sign of stupidity: they are side effects of shortcuts that usually serve us well. Knowing their names helps you notice them, though research suggests it doesn’t make you immune. Some famous findings in this field have failed to replicate, so the strength of the evidence is noted where it matters.
