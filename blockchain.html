Blockchain · consensus mechanisms

Agreement without anyone in charge.

A blockchain is a shared ledger that thousands of strangers keep identical copies of, with no bank or referee. Two ideas make it work: hashes that make tampering obvious, and a consensus rule that decides whose next page gets added.

Demo 1 · SHA-256, live

Edit any block and watch the chain break

Each block’s hash is a fingerprint of its contents and the previous block’s hash. Change one character in block 2 and its fingerprint changes, so block 3 no longer points to it, and so on down the chain. Re-mining means finding a new nonce whose hash starts with enough zeros: that work is proof of work.

Demo 2 · proof of work security

Can an attacker rewrite history?

To reverse a payment, an attacker must secretly build a longer chain than the honest network. Each confirmation is a block they have to make up. Satoshi Nakamoto’s 2008 white paper worked out the odds.

Chance the attacker ever catches up
Why six confirmations?With 10% of hash power, an attacker’s chance against 6 confirmations is about 0.02%. At 50% or more the attacker always wins eventually: that’s the “51% attack”. Smaller proof-of-work chains with little hash power, such as Ethereum Classic in 2020, have suffered real ones.
Demo 3 · fault tolerance

The Byzantine generals

Generals surrounding a city must all attack together or all wait, but some may be traitors sending conflicting messages. Lamport, Shostak and Pease (1982) proved that agreement needs more than two-thirds of the generals to be loyal. Proof-of-stake chains like Ethereum and BFT chains like Cosmos build on this result.

Protocol tolerates up to

Model: loyal generals vote ATTACK. Traitors tell half the others ATTACK and half RETREAT. A general commits once it hears a quorum (2f+1 matching votes, where f is the number of traitors the protocol was built to tolerate). This is the core of PBFT (Castro and Liskov, 1999) and Tendermint.
