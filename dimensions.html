Dimensions · and perception

You live in three dimensions. Probably.

We can’t picture a fourth spatial dimension, but we can reason about one, project it, and slice it, the same way a flat creature could reason about our world. And even the three we have reach us through senses that catch only a sliver of what’s there.

Demo 2 · Flatland

How a 2D creature would see your world

In Edwin Abbott’s 1884 novella Flatland, A Square is visited by a sphere. He can only see where it crosses his plane: a dot that grows into a circle and shrinks away. We would see a 4D object passing through our space the same way, as a 3D shape that appears, changes and vanishes.

Demo 3 · a visitor from the fourth dimension

What a higher-dimensional being would look like here

Now flip Flatland around. Imagine a being with one more spatial direction than us, which we’ll call W. Our whole 3D world is a thin slice of its space, and we only see the parts of it that cross our slice.

What you see in a sealed roomThe being, seen along W
What it could doAppear inside a locked room without touching the walls. See the inside of everything at once, the way you see every room on a floor plan. Lift an object out of a sealed box. Turn you around through W and set you back as your own mirror image, heart on the right.
And a fifth dimension?Each extra dimension repeats the trick one level up. If you count time as the fourth dimension, a five-dimensional being might take in our whole timeline at once, the way you can see every panel of a comic strip. Fiction loves this (the tesseract in Interstellar, Vonnegut’s Tralfamadorians in Slaughterhouse-Five), but there’s no evidence such beings or directions exist.
Demo 4 · physics

Why three dimensions might be special

In 3D, gravity spreads over a sphere’s surface and weakens as 1/r². In a world with n spatial dimensions it would weaken as 1/rⁿ⁻¹. In 1917 Paul Ehrenfest pointed out a consequence: with four or more dimensions, planets have no stable orbits.

Demo 5 · in-between dimensions

A shape with 1.26 dimensions

Dimension doesn’t have to be a whole number. The Koch snowflake (Helge von Koch, 1904) replaces every edge with four edges a third as long. Its outline grows forever while its area stays finite, so it’s more than a line but less than a surface.

Coastlines tooIn 1967 Benoit Mandelbrot asked “How long is the coast of Britain?” The answer depends on your ruler: the smaller it is, the longer the coast. Britain’s west coast behaves like a fractal of dimension about 1.25.
Demo 6 · perception

Your senses are a narrow window

Your brain doesn’t record the world; it builds a model of it from limited, noisy signals, and fills the gaps with assumptions. These experiments show the window, and the guesswork.

Same length?

The Müller-Lyer illusion (1889). One theory: we read the fins as the corners of rooms and buildings, and correct for depth that isn’t there.

Same grey?

Both squares are exactly the same grey (#808080). Your visual system judges brightness relative to the surroundings, which helps you see a white page as white in sunlight or shade.

What can you hear?

Young ears hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. The top end falls with age; many adults stop hearing somewhere between 14,000 and 17,000 Hz. Your speakers may also cut off before you do.

The electromagnetic spectrum: your eyes see the coloured sliver

Visible light is roughly one part in a trillion of the span shown here. Bees see ultraviolet, snakes sense infrared, and your phone talks in microwaves you’ll never perceive.
