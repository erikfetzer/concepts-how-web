Music & sound · waves you can hear

Every song is just air, wiggling.

Sound is a wave of pressure travelling through the air at about 343 metres per second. How fast it wiggles sets the pitch; how big the wiggles are sets the loudness; the mix of extra wiggles on top makes a flute sound different from a violin. Make waves, build instruments from harmonics, and hear why some notes sound good together. Turn your sound on, but keep the volume moderate.

Demo 1 · make a wave

Pitch, loudness and shape

A speaker cone pushes the air back and forth. The number of pushes per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). Double the frequency and the note goes up an octave. Change the wave’s shape and the same note gets a different character, or timbre.

Concert pitchThe A above middle C is tuned to 440 Hz, an international standard since 1955. Orchestras sometimes tune slightly higher, around 442 Hz, for a brighter sound.
Demo 2 · build an instrument

Harmonics: why a flute isn’t a violin

A real instrument plays a main note, the fundamental, plus quieter harmonics at 2, 3, 4… times its frequency. The recipe of harmonics is the instrument’s fingerprint. In 1822 Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating wave can be built this way. Drag the bars or pick a preset, then listen.

Odd harmonics onlyA clarinet is a tube closed at one end, which mostly allows odd harmonics: 1, 3, 5, 7… That gives it its hollow, woody tone. A square wave has the same recipe.
Demo 3 · harmony

Why some notes sound good together

Pythagoras is said to have noticed that pleasant intervals come from simple ratios of string lengths: 2:1 for an octave, 3:2 for a fifth. When two frequencies fit a simple ratio, their waves line up often and the combined pattern repeats quickly. Tap the keyboard or pick an interval.

Simple ratio
On a piano

The piano’s compromiseSimple ratios don’t fit together perfectly around the circle of keys. Modern pianos use equal temperament: every semitone multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of 2 (about 1.0595). Every key works equally well, and almost every interval is very slightly out of tune.
Demo 4 · interference

Beats: hearing two notes fight

Play two notes that are almost, but not quite, the same. Their waves drift in and out of step, so the sound swells and fades: “wah-wah-wah”. The number of beats per second equals the difference between the two frequencies. Musicians tune by making the beats slow down until they disappear.

The same interference makes noise-cancelling headphones work: they play a sound that is the exact opposite of the noise, so the two waves cancel out.

Demo 5 · moving sounds

The Doppler effect

An ambulance siren sounds higher as it comes towards you and lower as it drives away. The waves ahead of it get squashed together and the ones behind get stretched. Christian Doppler predicted this in 1842; in 1845 it was tested with trumpeters on a moving train. Push the speed past the speed of sound and the waves pile up into a shock wave: a sonic boom.

Not just soundLight does it too. Galaxies moving away from us look redder: the redshift that revealed the universe is expanding. Police radar and weather radar use the Doppler effect to measure speed.
Demo 6 · your ears

How high can you hear?

Young people can hear from about 20 Hz up to 20,000 Hz. With age, and with loud noise, the top of the range drops. Start low and work up; stop when you can’t hear anything. Keep the volume low: high tones can be unpleasant. Phone and laptop speakers may not play the highest ones at all.

Each tone plays for one second, quietly.

How loud is loud? (decibels)
Decibels multiplyEvery 10 dB is ten times more sound energy, so 100 dB is a billion times more intense than 10 dB. Long exposure above about 85 dB, like heavy traffic or loud headphones, can damage hearing permanently.
