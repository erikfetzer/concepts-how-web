Time paradoxes · physics & philosophy

Two clocks disagree. Both are right.

Einstein showed that elapsed time depends on how you move. Philosophers had been arguing about whether time flows at all for two thousand years. Here are the puzzles that came out of both, with demos you can run.

Demo 3 · thermodynamics

The arrow of time (Loschmidt’s paradox)

Newton’s laws work the same forwards and backwards, yet eggs break and never unbreak. In 1876 Josef Loschmidt challenged Ludwig Boltzmann: if every collision is reversible, how can entropy only increase?

Try itLet the gas spread, then reverse every velocity. Each particle exactly retraces its path and the gas regathers in the corner. The laws allow it; you just never see it, because almost every possible arrangement is a spread-out one.

The usual answer: the arrow of time comes from the starting conditions, not the laws. The universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, and we live on the long slide away from it. Why it started that way is still open.

Andromeda paradox

Penrose, 1989

Two people walk past each other on Earth. For one, the Andromeda fleet’s launch decision is already “in the past”; for the other it hasn’t been made yet.

At 2.5 million light-years, even walking speed tilts your line of “now” by days on Andromeda. Based on arguments by Rietdijk (1966) and Putnam (1967).

TakeawayThere is no universal present. Some take this as evidence that past, present and future all exist equally (see “Does time flow?” below).

Clocks on planes

Hafele–Keating, 1971

Is time dilation real or a trick of bookkeeping?

Physicists flew cesium atomic clocks around the world in both directions. The eastbound clocks lost about 59 nanoseconds and the westbound gained about 273, matching relativity’s predictions.

TodayGPS satellite clocks run about 38 microseconds a day fast (gravity speeds them up more than motion slows them), and the system corrects for it. Without that, positions would drift by kilometers a day.

Closed timelike curves

Gödel, 1949

Does general relativity allow travel to your own past?

Kurt Gödel found a solution to Einstein’s equations, a rotating universe, where you can loop back to your own past. Wormhole versions followed (Morris, Thorne and Yurtsever, 1988).

StatusThe equations permit them, but every known version needs conditions nobody has seen, such as exotic negative energy. That opens the door to the paradoxes below.

Demo 4 · infinity

Zeno’s Achilles and the tortoise

Zeno of Elea (5th century BC) argued that swift Achilles can never catch a tortoise with a head start. By the time he reaches where it was, it has moved on; by the time he reaches that spot, it has moved again, forever. Here Achilles runs 10 m/s and the tortoise 1 m/s with a 100 m lead.

The modern answerInfinitely many stages can fit in finite time: 10 + 1 + 0.1 + 0.01 + … seconds adds up to exactly 11.11… seconds. The tools to sum such series rigorously (limits) arrived in the 1800s. Some philosophers still argue that the maths describes the race without explaining how anything completes infinitely many steps.

His dichotomy paradox is the same trick backwards: to cross a room you must first cross half, and half of that, so how do you ever start? His arrow paradox asks how a flying arrow moves if at every instant it is motionless.

Augustine’s puzzle

c. 400 AD

“What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it, I do not know.”

In Confessions Book XI, Augustine notes that the past no longer exists and the future doesn’t exist yet, while the present has no length. So how can we measure a stretch of time at all?

His answerTime lives in the mind: memory holds the past, expectation the future, attention the present.

McTaggart’s unreality of time

1908

Can an event be past, present and future at once?

J. M. E. McTaggart split time into the A-series (past, present, future, which keep changing) and the B-series (earlier and later, which never change). He argued change needs the A-series, but the A-series is contradictory, since every event has all three properties.

LegacyMost philosophers reject his conclusion that time is unreal, but his A/B split still frames the debate below.

The sea battle

Aristotle, 4th c. BC

Is “there will be a sea battle tomorrow” already true or false today?

If it is already true, the battle seems unavoidable, and fatalism follows. If it’s neither, logic’s rule that every statement is true or false fails.

OpenAristotle seems to say future contingents aren’t yet true or false. Logicians still debate it under the name “future contingents”.

The unexpected hanging

in print 1948

A judge says you’ll be hanged on a weekday next week, and it will be a surprise.

You reason it can’t be Friday (you’d know Thursday night), so not Thursday either, and so on, concluding it can’t happen. Then you’re hanged on Wednesday, completely surprised.

Why it’s hereIt shows how knowledge about the future can undercut itself. Martin Gardner popularized it in 1963, and there’s still no agreed fix.

Theory

Bootstrap paradox

Heinlein, 1941

A traveler hands young Shakespeare a copy of Hamlet, which Shakespeare then publishes. Who wrote it?

Information or objects circle in a closed loop with no origin. Named after Robert Heinlein’s story “By His Bootstraps”.

Why it bothers peopleNothing contradicts itself, which makes it harder to dismiss than the grandfather paradox. It just creates something from nothing.

Polchinski’s billiard ball

c. 1990

A ball enters a wormhole, exits in the past, and knocks its younger self off course so it never enters.

Physicist Joseph Polchinski posed this grandfather paradox without free will. Echeverria, Klinkhammer and Thorne (1991) found that every starting shot they tried had at least one self-consistent path, often glancing blows, and sometimes infinitely many.

TakeawayPhysics may simply have no paradoxical solutions, just surprising consistent ones.

Predestination paradox

ancient

Trying to prevent a prophecy is what fulfils it.

Oedipus’s parents abandon him to avoid a prophecy, which sets up its fulfilment. Time-travel stories use the same loop: the attempt to change history is part of history.

LinkThis is Novikov’s self-consistency principle told as tragedy.

Where are the tourists?

Hawking, 1992

If time travel is ever invented, why haven’t visitors from the future shown up?

Stephen Hawking proposed a chronology protection conjecture: physics, likely quantum effects, prevents time machines. In 2009 he threw a party for time travelers and sent the invitations only afterwards. Nobody came.

CaveatMany proposals only allow travel back to when the machine was built, which would also explain the empty party.
Big picture

Does time flow? Three answers

Relativity’s lack of a shared “now” pushes many physicists toward the block universe, but the debate is philosophical, not settled by experiment.

Presentism

Only the present exists. The past is gone, the future isn’t real yet. Matches experience, but clashes with relativity, where observers disagree about what “present” means.

Growing block

The past and present exist; the future doesn’t yet. Reality grows as new moments are added. Defended by C. D. Broad (1923) and others.

Eternalism (block universe)

Past, present and future all exist, like places on a map. “Now” is just where you are, the way “here” is. Fits relativity neatly, but makes the feeling of flow hard to explain.
